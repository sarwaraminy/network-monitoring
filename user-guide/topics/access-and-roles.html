Who can do what

Two roles, and the line between them.

Every account has one of two roles. There is no per-account partitioning of data below that: findings, devices and rules are properties of the network being watched, not of the person looking, so two accounts of the same role see exactly the same thing.

RoleIntended for
USERSomebody who reads findings and works through them: sees everything the tool has found, and can mark a finding as dealt with.
ADMINSomebody responsible for the installation: everything a USER can do, plus every action that changes configuration, destroys data, or causes the server to reach out to something.

A screen a role cannot use is hidden from the navigation panel rather than shown and refused. The restriction is enforced by the server, not by hiding the link: an account that reaches an administrator-only address directly is refused there too.

What is never shown to anybody

Some values are write-only regardless of role, because the screens that display them are readable by every signed-in account:

  • The webhook URL — for Slack and Teams that URL is the credential.
  • The SMTP password, the OAuth2 client secret and the OAuth2 refresh token.

Each is reported as Configured or Not set and can be replaced or cleared, never read back. The OAuth2 client ID is deliberately not on that list: it identifies the application rather than authenticating it, and hiding it would leave the form unable to show which registration is in use.

The audit trail records which fields a settings change touched, never their values — a trail holding credentials would be a second place to read them, one that is never pruned.

This guide is behind the same door

The pages you are reading are served by the application itself and require a signed-in session, the same as any screen. Opening one without signing in lands on the sign-in page, and the screenshots and stylesheet are refused outright — they show a real dashboard, a delivery configuration and the shape of an alerts table, which is not something to leave readable by anybody who guesses a URL.

The session for the guide is a separate, short-lived cookie scoped to /user-guide, because a page a browser navigates to cannot carry the token the application uses for everything else. Signing out clears it, and a guide tab left open returns itself to the sign-in page when it notices you have signed out elsewhere.

Changing somebody's role

Administration settings → Users and roles. Pick a role on the account's row; it applies at once, and the change is written to the audit trail with who made it and which way the role moved.

Two changes are refused, and the interface disables them rather than letting you find out by being told no:

  • You cannot change your own role. Ask another administrator. If it were allowed, the click would remove your access to the page you are standing on.
  • The last administrator cannot be demoted. Promote a second account first. Otherwise every administrative function — including this one — would be behind the door that just closed, recoverable only from a shell on the server.

With only one administrator you will see a standing reminder to promote a second. It is not about lockout so much as recovery: with one administrator, a forgotten password means editing the database by hand.

Note

Roles are checked against the account as it exists at the moment of the request, not against what a session was issued with. Changing an account's role takes effect on that account's next request, and does not wait for them to sign in again.
